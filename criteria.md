# Acceptance criteria — Provenance Guard

Five criteria that say what "working" means for this service, written in unit 7
**before** you built it.

**All five are yours.** None are given.

An acceptance criterion names a number or something plainly observable. *"The
detector is accurate"* is an opinion — and perfect AI detection isn't possible
anyway, so it's an opinion about something nobody can deliver. *"Of 10 pieces I
wrote myself, at most 1 is labelled high-confidence AI"* is a criterion.

Under each, write a sentence or two on **why that target**. *"I allow at most 1
false positive in 10 because on a writing site a wrong accusation costs more
than a missed detection"* is a real answer.

> Missing your own targets next unit costs you nothing. Setting a target so
> easy you can't miss it does.

---

## Pick numbers you can defend

**Cover at least three of these five areas.** They're prompts, not a form.

| Area | A question it could answer |
|---|---|
| False positives | How often may a human's writing get called AI? |
| Score spread | How far apart should clearly-AI and clearly-human text score? |
| Label coverage | Is every one of your three labels actually reachable? |
| The appeal path | Does an appeal always change status and always get recorded? |
| Bad input | What happens when `text` is missing, empty, or enormous? |

Two things worth knowing before you pick numbers:

- **The expensive mistake is one-sided.** Missing some AI text costs the site a
  little. Accusing a real writer costs that person a lot. A criterion that
  treats both errors as equally bad is describing a system nobody would ship.
- **Unit 8 tests across three trials, and the target has to hold across all
  three.** Your first signal runs a model, so identical input can score
  differently twice. A target of "at most 1 in 10" against trials of 2, 1, 2 is
  a **miss**.

---
## 1. My own writing is almost never called AI

Of 10 pieces I wrote myself, at most 1 gets the high-confidence AI label. This has to hold in each of the three trials, and at least 3 of the 10 must be plain, short-sentence writing, since that is the kind most likely to be accused.

**Why this target:** On a writing site a wrong accusation costs one person a lot and a missed detection costs the site a little, so I let the false-positive count be small and the miss count be larger. I can't ask for 0 in 10 because both signals read plain human writing as machine-like (see DESIGN.md), and a target I can't plausibly hit tells me nothing. I include the plain pieces on purpose, because a set of only ornate writing would pass without testing the case that worries me.

---

## 2. Clearly-AI and clearly-human text score far apart

Take 5 pieces that are clearly AI (generated by a chatbot with no edits) and 5 that are clearly human (written by me, with uneven sentences and specific detail). In each trial, the average combined score of the AI group is at least 0.30 higher than the average of the human group, and the lowest AI score is above the highest human score.

**Why this target:** The two thresholds sit 0.30 apart (0.35 and 0.65), so a gap of at least 0.30 between the groups means the typical clear case can fall into different labels. A smaller gap means the signals aren't separating anything and the labels are decoration. I add the no-overlap condition because averages can hide one AI text scoring as human.

---

## 3. All three labels are reachable

Across a set of 12 inputs chosen to span the score range, each of the three labels (human, unsure, AI) is produced by at least 2 inputs, in each of the three trials.

**Why this target:** Config comments warn that a style signal stuck at 0.5 makes the human label unreachable, and a label no input can produce is dead text. Requiring 2 per label, not 1, stops a single borderline input from carrying a label by luck, and checking in all three trials catches a label that is only reachable some of the time. The unsure label matters most, since it is what protects a plain writer from the AI label.

---

## 4. An appeal always changes status and always gets recorded

For 5 real `content_id`s returned by earlier submissions, every appeal returns 200 with status `under_review`, and every one adds an `appeal` entry to the log while the original decision entry stays unchanged. An appeal with an unknown `content_id` returns 404 and adds no appeal entry. That is 6 out of 6, in each trial, with no exceptions.

**Why this target:** The appeal is the only thing a wrongly accused writer has. If it silently fails once, that person gets nothing, so I allow no misses. This is also fully deterministic and doesn't depend on the model, so a perfect target is fair here. It is not an easy one: a typo in the id lookup is the most common way this endpoint breaks.

---

## 5. Bad input gets a clear error, never a crash

Send 6 malformed submissions: no `text` field, empty text, whitespace-only text, `text` as a number, `text` of 50,000 characters, and a body that is a JSON list instead of an object. Every one returns a 4xx status with a JSON `error` code, none returns a 500, and `/ping` still answers afterwards.

**Why this target:** A crash on malformed input is one of the most common real failures, and a 500 here means my handler broke on something a caller could send on purpose. Checking `/ping` afterwards separates "my handler failed" from "the service died", the way app.py suggests. Six cases because each one reaches a different line of validation, so one passing says nothing about the others.
